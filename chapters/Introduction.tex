\chapter{Introduction}

It can hardly have by-passed many that the field of machine learning has seen an immense growth in popularity in the past decade, not only in terms of research productivity\footnote{Publishing Statistics: \href{https://papercopilot.com/statistics/neurips-statistics/}{Neurips}, \href{https://papercopilot.com/statistics/icml-statistics/}{ICML}, \href{https://papercopilot.com/statistics/iclr-statistics/}{ICLR}.}, but equally in applications and popular media \citep{2018_ukmediacoverage, 2021_industryapps}. The recent announcement of Geoffrey Hinton, John Hopfield, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper as Nobel Prize laureates in classical science disciplines is a further testament to this. The popularisation of, and developments on, neural networks is perhaps the largest contributor and reason for this explosion in machine learning interest. These models have shown immense success in many applications ranging from text  \citep{2023_GPT3} and image generation \citep{2022_stablediffusion} to assisting diagnostics in hospitals \citep{2019_breastcancerassistance,2022_mlinhealthcarereview}, to being used in self-driving cars \citep{2017_selfdrivingcars,2021_selfdrivingcarssurvey}.\\

\noindent Regardless of their immense functional flexibility and their universal approximation properties \citep{1989_cybenkouniversal, 1989_hornikuniversal}, neural networks are still seeing slow adaption in some industries, in particular in safety critical applications or even just in live systems where uncertainty quantification is of immense importance. This is in part due to the fact that standard neural network training methods often suffer from poor uncertainty quantification \citep{2017_calibmodernnn}. Examples of safety critical applications include diagnostic assistance where if a first screening is completed by a machine learning model, it is possible to refer to a doctor for a second opinion if the model confidence is low, whilst high confidence diagnostics may be relied on \citep{2021_usinguncertaintyhealthcare}. In live systems where data quality at times is bad (and sometimes even completely incorrect), model uncertainty can be used to flag data with poor quality \citep{2024_mriuncertainty}. However, for such systems to be robust, the used models must not only have inherent uncertainty quantification - it is also necessary for the models to be \textit{well-calibrated}. Throughout this dissertation I will often discuss the concept of \textit{calibration}. For now, it suffices to think that the model must not only have predictive uncertainty, but that the uncertainty should also reflect the predictive performance of the model. In other words, a \textit{perfectly calibrated} model is a model in which the predictive uncertainty exactly matches the empirical performance of the model. \\

\noindent There exists a vast amount of examples where model uncertainty quantification and calibration plays an important role. There also exists several ways of performing uncertainty quantification such as using generative models to perform out-of-distribution detection \citep{2019_nalisnickooddetection, 2021_hiervaesood, 2022_bergaminood}, yet in this dissertation we are specifically interested in obtaining good \textit{predictive uncertainty} in supervised tasks. Such predictive uncertainty can be useful in a variety of applications, some of which are perhaps more intuitive than others. An obvious and intuitive case, is that previously mentioned of using predictive uncertainty either for sample rejection or referral to humans in human-in-the-loop systems. More concretely, one can consider the hospital diagnostic setting. In hospital diagnostics, it is not uncommon that if a less experienced doctor or radiologist is unsure of the diagnose based on some x-ray image, they ask a more experienced doctor for a second opinion. Similarly, in diagnostic systems where machine learning models are adopted, we can use predictive uncertainty to refer to a human for a second opinion if the predictive uncertainty is too high. Specifically, one can set a confidence threshold for the predictive uncertainty of the model for some diagnostic case, and if the predicted uncertainty is higher than this threshold, then a human must also evaluate the sample. Another case is that of financial modelling. If a machine learning model is used for predicting the developments of a specific stock price in the future, the predictive uncertainty can be used to hedge or adjust the amount invested into a stock. For example, if predictive uncertainty of the stock price is high (high risk), but the potential payout is low (low reward), then such investments can be rejected (for a survey on uncertainty quantification in financial modelling see \citep{2024_financialmodelling}. Finally, there are less intuitive but nevertheless equally useful use cases, e.g. sequential experimental design such as active learning \citep{1994_activelearning} or Bayesian Optimisation \citep{2001_BO, 2012_BO}. In active learning, the predictive uncertainty is used via some data fit measure of potential new points at which to sample for the most informative update of the model, whilst in Bayesian Optimisation the relationship between predictive mean and uncertainty is used to choose the degree to which one wishes to exploit or explore the input space in order to efficiently optimise some black-box function. \\

\noindent Based on the previously listed applications, it should be clear that exactly how uncertainty is quantified is dependent on the task at hand. Throughout this dissertation we consider predictive uncertainty in the context of classification or regression tasks. For neural networks, there is much work being done on how to improve the predictive uncertainty. Some examples of research areas include implicit regularization during training \citep{2017_focalloss, 2020_focalloss}, data augmentations \citep{2019_mixup, 2019_labelsmoothing} or post-hoc methods such as temperature scaling \citep{2017_calibmodernnn} or conformal predictions \citep{1998_conformalpred, 1999_conformalpred}. Finally there is the research area of applying the Bayesian Statistics toolbox to neural networks, commonly referred to as Bayesian Neural Networks \citep{1992_MacKay, 2003_MacKayBNN, 2015_BayesByBack, 2019_SWAG}. The main motivation for this area, is that it is obvious that in the finite data setting (the only one we still have for most modelling tasks) and with large and over-parametrised neural networks, there exist many optimal solutions which fit our data, and thus taking these solutions into account at prediction time should, in principle, significantly improve uncertainty quantification. Most of the work throughout this dissertation follows the probabilistic and Bayesian approach to modelling, to improve uncertainty quantification. The pattern is clear: neural networks often do not have good uncertainty quantification out-of-the-box, and there is therefore much work that concerns itself with improving this, as it is a key aspect in many important downstream applications of neural networks. \\

\noindent On the contrary to neural networks, which in the grand scheme of things can be considered "easy" to scale but difficult to impose good uncertainty quantification behaviours onto, there exists a class of models originally known as "Kriging" as developed by Georges Matheron in the 1960's for application in geostatistics \citep{1963_matheron} - today commonly known as Gaussian Processes. I say on the contrary because they, unlike most commonly used neural networks, have very good inherent uncertainty quantification and are most-often \textit{well-calibrated} (at least if kernel and prior are specified \textit{somewhat} appropriately) whilst they suffer from their ability to scale and lower functional flexibility - at least in their standard formulation. Gaussian Processes have been immensely popular within the field of machine learning since the 1970's in large part due to their inherent uncertainty quantification, but perhaps equally so due to the elegance of the underlying theory and mathematics, and principles that govern them, i.e. Bayesian probability theory. It is perhaps also for this reason that many of those who worked heavily with Gaussian Processes in the past are now looking at applying Bayesian principles to neural networks for better uncertainty quantification. In fact, there are also many works that concern themselves with connecting the theory of GPs and neural networks for better understanding and uncertainty quantification in neural networks \citep{Lee2017DeepNN, NEURIPS2018_5a4be1fa, ApproximateInferenceasGPs}. Nevertheless, GPs have remained dominant in paradigms where uncertainty quantification is of utmost importance and data is sparse such as financial modelling \citep{Gonzalvez2019FinancialAO} and Bayesian Optimisation, but with recent improvements on Bayesian Neural Networks the gap appears to be closing \citep{li2024a}. This could be a sign that Bayesian Neural Networks potentially soon could be competitive with Gaussian Processes in terms of uncertainty quantification in certain applications.\\

\noindent Throughout my PhD, I have completed research on both methodological and applied problems. The methodological papers concern themselves with  \textit{how to obtain calibrated neural networks}, whilst the applied papers concern themselves with \textit{why it matters} having calibrated models. Paper A and Paper B fall under methodological proposals and questions. Paper A I completed during my external stay at Universidad Carlos III de Madrid with Pablo Olmos and was published at ICML 2024. Here we showed that retraining the classification layers in a neural network significantly improves calibration of the neural network at no cost of accuracy - and it was in fact an accidental discovery (more on this later). Paper B was completed at DTU and is currently under review at AISTATS. In this work we investigated how including local posterior structure in deep ensembles impacts predictive uncertainty. On the more applied side there is firstly Paper C which was published at UAI 2023. Here we completed a large empirical study to show the effect of model calibration on Bayesian Optimisation performance. Paper D is also related to Bayesian Optimisation and looks at using newly proposed Variational Bayesian Last Layer neural networks as surrogate models and is currently under review at ICLR (a smaller version was accepted at the NeurIPS workshop on Bayesian Decision-making and Uncertainty). Papers E and F are both focused on a specific educational technology (ed-tech) application called WriteReader\footnote{\url{https://www.writereader.com/}.} - Paper E was published at NLDL 2024 and Paper F is currently available as a pre-print. In these papers we investigate how to obtain calibrated Transformer models for this specific application, but more importantly show that if the models are well calibrated, the models can further improve the ed-tech application.

\section{Outline and Research Questions}

I have throughout my brief research career always aspired to do research, not simply because \textit{I could}, but rather to do research because \textit{there was a reason to}. Whether I have succeeded in this or not, I will leave up to others to judge. However, as time went by, it became clear that establishing actual research projects in real healthcare scenarios was far from trivial, and for this reason some of my projects ended up being focused on general methodologies for obtaining calibrated neural networks which \textit{in principle} could be used in healthcare scenarios. In my more applied projects I have tried to illustrate how when we obtain calibrated models with good uncertainty quantification, we can use them to improve downstream applications. Consequently, the red line throughout this dissertation is about calibrated machine learning models and how to use them, underlined by the title \textit{Calibrated Machine Learning Models: How to Get Them and Why They Matter}. \\

\noindent \textbf{Research Question 1:} \textit{Does optimising classification and feature extraction layers at the same time lead to poorly calibrated neural networks and does imposing structure on the feature embedding space further improve calibration?}\\

\noindent The truth is that this research question was completely post rationalised, because the original research question we set out to answer was: \textit{Can we specify and train a class of generative classifiers to be more robust to adversarial attacks than a discriminative classifier?} Both \cite{li2019are} and \cite{Schott2018TowardsTF} tried this back in 2018/2019 with some success, but have recently been largely out-competed by other model types \citep{pmlr-v202-wang23ad,bartoldson2024adversarial}. The problem with using VAE-based generative classifier is that the clean accuracy of the model falls drastically. With this in mind, our idea was to specify a variational family that was more rich and could encompass pre-trained models - yet after several months, we had little success. In frustration, I decided to evaluate our proposed model using the typical metrics used when evaluating calibration and to our surprise found that the models were performing extremely well! As such Paper A came to life due to an accidental discovery after a number of ablation experiments. \\

\noindent In Paper A, we show that training neural networks (in particular Wide Residual Networks and Vision Transformers) in two-stages for classification tasks \textit{significantly} improves calibration of the model - leading to two proposed methods Two-Stage Training (TST) and Variational Two-Stage Training (V-TST). In Stage 1, both the backbone and logits layer are trained until convergence (or early stopped), after which the logits layer is re-initialised and the backbone is frozen. In Stage 2, the newly initialised logits layer is then trained using \textit{using the same training data and loss} until early-stopped based on validation negative log-likelihood. We provide a large amount of empirical experiments and ablation studies to solidify this finding, as well as some intuition on why we think this happens. Finally we also show that probabilistically modelling the final hidden layer output of the model and training with the evidence lower-bound further improves calibration of the model. \\

 \noindent \textbf{Research Question 2:} \textit{Does local posterior structure improve Deep Ensembles?}\\

\noindent In recent years, Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) have been receiving increasing attention and a variety of approximate inference methods for such networks have been proposed. It is widely accepted and has been shown that most of these methods improve on uncertainty quantification and calibration of otherwise MAP estimated neural networks. However, many of these methods just approximate the posterior in the region of a single mode, which we know is a crude approximation since the true posterior of overparameterised neural networks are highly multi modal. Deep Ensembles (DEs) is one of a few Bayesian inference methods that can actually cover multiple modes of the posterior, yet do so only with only point estimates at the modes. It is therefore a natural hypothesis that describing local posterior structure at each of these modes and marginalizing over them at prediction time should further improve uncertainty quantification and calibration.\\

\noindent In Paper B, we systematically investigate if including local posterior structure in DEs further improves uncertainty quantification and calibration. We investigate this across a wide range of datasets, neural network architectures and ensemble size to solidify our findings empirically. \cite{2021_MOLA} and \cite{2020_MultiSWAG} already experimented with this, but our work provide additional findings across a larger class of data modalities, ensemble sizes and architectures, which shines a new light upon ensembles of BNNs.\\

\noindent \textbf{Research Question 3:} \textit{To what degree does surrogate model uncertainty matter in Bayesian Optimisation?}\\

\noindent In Bayesian Optimisation (BO), it is widely known and understood that the predictive uncertainty of the used surrogate model plays a role in the overall performance of the BO routine. This can be directly seen from observing the relationship between model predictive uncertainty and mean in the used acquisition functions, and yet, the degree \textit{to which} model uncertainty matters had not previously been investigated thoroughly. Based on the observation that model calibration matters for BO performance, \cite{recalibration_BO} also proposed a method for re-calibrating the predictive uncertainties of the surrogate model during a BO session, yet it was not clear if such methods are guaranteed to work\footnote{The Deshpande paper has received several updates since our UAI paper was published.}. \\

\noindent In Paper C we empirically investigated the degree to which surrogate model calibration matters for BO performance across a large number of surrogate models, acquisition functions and optimization problems. We also performed an intervention study to see if over-confidence or under-confidence played a larger impact on BO performance, and finally showed both theoretically and empirically that the re-calibration method proposed in \cite{recalibration_BO} in fact may lead to \textit{worse} BO performance. Based on these experiments and results we presented advice for how to perform BO well to practitioners.\\

\noindent \textbf{Research Question 4:} \textit{How do Variational Bayesian Last Layer (VBLL) neural networks perform as surrogates in Bayesian Optimisation and how can we improve fitting time of these surrogates in this setting?} \\

\noindent Gaussian Processes (GP) have for a long time been the \textit{de-facto} surrogate model used in Bayesian Optimisation, in large part due to their inherently good uncertainty quantification in small-data regimes. However, for some optimisation problems, one cannot simply rely on classical GP kernels and must design bespoke kernels. Unfortunately, designing such kernels for high-dimensional, non-stationary, potentially multi-output and structured problems is highly non-trivial. Neural networks on the other hand are well suited for these problem types, yet classically lack good uncertainty quantification. As a response to this, Bayesian Neural Networks have seen larger and larger interest in the BO community \citep{2015_BLL, springenberg2016bayesian, li2024a}. \\

\noindent In Paper D we investigate the use of the recently proposed Variational Bayesian Last Layer neural networks \citep{2024_VBLL} for usage as surrogate models in Bayesian Optimisation. We firstly bench-marked the VBLL models against GP and BNN surrogates that have previously been seen as state of the art surrogate models for certain structured problems, on a large number of problems. We then investigated VBLL hyperparameter sensitivity and showed how certain acquisition functions fit these models particularly well. Finally, we presented a novel continual learning scheme for VBLLs to reduce surrogate fit times. \\



\noindent \textbf{Research Question 5:} \textit{How can we improve e-learning by utilising probabilistic and Bayesian modelling?}\\

\noindent With the recent developments and interest in Large Language Models (LLMs), it is not surprising that such models are being considered for utilisation in e-learning platforms. WriteReader is one such platform, and they have been particularly interested in partly automating feedback on children's early stage writing\footnote{Writing here does not mean the physical act of writing, but rather writing sentences on a computer. We say "translating" writing because we are not interested in grammatically correct writing (i.e. it is not a grammatical "correction"), but rather interested in understanding the \textit{intended} writing and use of language presented in the writing.}. To do so, WriteReader wishes to utilise natural language processing methods for "translating" young children's writing to teacher writing. \\

\noindent In Papers E and F we developed Transformers \citep{NIPS2017_3f5ee243} for the WriteReader platform and experimented with how the uncertainty quantification can be utilised in the WriteReader application. In Paper E we looked at developing the models for English language, and in particular looked at how we could adapt typical fine-tuning procedures to improve training as well as utilising BNN methods for these models to improve model calibration. In Paper F we looked at developing models for Danish, a language where pre-trained LLMs are not available and we therefore looked at how to train robust and well-calibrated Transformers from scratch. In both papers we looked at various ways of utilising the uncertainty quantification of the trained Transformer models in the WriteReader platform to improve the application.
